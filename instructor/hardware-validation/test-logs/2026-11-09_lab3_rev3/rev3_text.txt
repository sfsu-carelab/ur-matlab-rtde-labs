Lab 3
Forward Kinematics of UR e-series Robots via Screw Theory (Product of Exponentials)
Accompanying MATLAB file(s):  UR_FK_Lab.m, fk_space.m, ur_link_lengths.m, screw2hw.m, hw2screw.m, run_self_tests.p
Overview
In this lab you will derive the forward kinematics of a Universal Robots e-series 6-DOF manipulator (worked example: UR3e) using the Product of Exponentials (PoE) formulation from screw theory. Forward kinematics relates the values of the joint angles to the pose of the end effector, meaning its orientation and its Cartesian position.
The overall objective is to derive the kinematic parameters (the six screw axes and the home pose matrix) from the robot's geometry, enter them into the MATLAB file, and verify the results against the physical robot.
Learning Objectives
Identify the rotation axis ω and a point q on that axis for every joint of a 6-DOF manipulator from its physical geometry.
Compute the 6x1 screw axis S=(ω,v) for each joint using v=-ω×q.
Assemble the home pose matrix M from the robot's dimensions.
Compute the final homogeneous transformation matrix T.
Verify your Forward Kinematics (FK) model against the physical robot.
Background Theory: The Product of Exponentials Formula
For a kinematic-chain manipulator with n revolute joints, the space-frame PoE formula gives the end-effector pose as:
T(θ)=e[S1]θ1e[S2]θ2⋯e[S6]θ6M
Each factor e[Si]θi is a 4x4 transformation matrix that represents the effect of rotating joint i by angle θi. When θi is zero, that factor reduces to the 4x4 identity matrix. When all joint angles are set to zero, every exponential is the identity, so T=M.
The quantities you need to determine are:
M: the 4x4 end-effector pose at the home zero configuration of the robot (all joint angles zero).
ωi: a 3x1 unit vector along the rotation axis of joint i.
qi: any point on the rotation axis of joint i.
vi=-ωi×qi: the linear velocity component, computed from ω and q via a cross product.
Si=[ωi;vi]: the 6x1 screw axis of joint i in the space frame at the home zero configuration.
The math that converts S and θ into a 4x4 matrix from e[Si]θi (building off the matrix exponential e[ωi]θi, using Rodrigues' formula) is provided for you in the MATLAB file. The task is to figure out the correct Si and M from the robot's geometry.

Worked Example: The Screw Axis of Joint 2 (Shoulder)
Before filling in the table, let us derive one screw axis completely. We will use joint 2, the shoulder; every other joint follows the same three steps.
Step 1 - Find the rotation axis ω2.  In the home configuration (Figure 1), the red arrow of joint 2 is horizontal and points along the −y direction of the base frame. Since ω must be a unit vector: ω2=(0,-1,0).
Step 2 - Pick a point q2 on the axis.  The shoulder axis crosses the base z-axis at height L1 above the origin, so a convenient choice is q2=(0,0,L1)=(0,0,0.15185) m for the UR3e. Any point on the axis will work; a convenient one keeps the arithmetic simple.
Step 3 - Compute v2=-ω2×q2.  Writing the cross product out component by component:
-ω2×q2=-(0,-1,0)×(0,0,L1)=(0,1,0)×(0,0,L1)
=((1)(L1)-(0)(0))i-((0)(L1)-(0)(0))j+((0)(0)-(1)(0))k=(L1)i-(0)j+(0)k
so v2=(L1,0,0)=(0.15185,0,0).
Stacking ω on top of v gives the 6x1 screw axis vector:  S2=(ω2,v2)=[0,-1,0,L1,0,0]=[0,-1,0,0.15185,0,0]. The symbolic form is what you will enter in MATLAB:  [0, -1, 0, L1, 0, 0].
Physical check: v is the velocity of the point of the moving body that currently sits at the base origin. If the shoulder rotates at 1 rad/s about −y, a point at the origin (a distance L1 below the axis) sweeps in the +x direction at L1 m/s, which is exactly v2=(L1,0,0).
Check your understanding: repeat Step 3 with a different point on the same axis, for example q=(0,0.3,L1). The extra y component is parallel to ω2, so it drops out of the cross product and v2 is unchanged. If choosing a different point on the axis changes your v, something has gone wrong.
General cross product formula. For any two vectors a=(a1,a2,a3) and b=(b1,b2,b3): a×b=(a2b3-a3b2)i-(a1b3-a3b1)j+(a1b2-a2b1)k. The worked example above is this formula with a=-ω and b=q. You will need it with ω along −y (joints 2, 3, 4 and 6) and along +z (joints 1 and 5).
Now derive the remaining five screw axes the same way and record them in Table 2. Joints 3, 4 and 6 are directly analogous to joint 2: ω=(0,-1,0) and q on the base z-axis at the height of that joint. Read the heights from Figure 1: joint 3 is L1+L2 up, joint 4 is L1+L2+L3 up, and joint 6 is a further L5 above joint 4. Joint 1 is simpler still: its axis passes through the origin, so q=(0,0,0) and v=(0,0,0). Joint 5 is the only axis that does not cross the base z-axis: it is vertical and sits a distance L4 out along −y, so its q has a nonzero y component.

Part 4 - Hardware Verification
In this part you compare your FK model with the real robot. You jog the robot to a few poses with the Teach Pendant, read its joint angles and its tool position, and check that fk_space predicts the same position from the same angles.
⚠️ Before moving the robot: set the speed slider to 25%, clear the workspace, and keep the e-stop within reach. In this lab the robot is only jogged from the Teach Pendant; nothing is commanded from MATLAB.
If your group runs out of time on the robot, the same steps can be finished in URSim, the Universal Robots simulator, on your own computer. Its Teach Pendant screen shows the same joint angles and TCP position. Expect an error close to zero there, because the simulator uses the nominal link lengths.
Task 3: Map the Pendant Angles to Screw Angles
The screw model measures every joint angle from the upright home configuration of Figure 1, where all six screw angles are zero. The Teach Pendant measures from a different zero. At that same upright pose it reads:
theta_home = [0, -90, 0, -90, 0, 0]  (degrees)
The rotation directions are the same in both (the red arrows of Figure 1), so the two sets of angles differ only by this constant offset:
θhw=θscrew+θhome        θscrew=θhw-θhome
The helper functions hw2screw and screw2hw apply these two formulas, with theta_home built in. All angles are in degrees. The robot does not need to be moved to the upright pose.
Check your model on one worked pose before going to the robot. Suppose the pendant shows the joint angles [30, −60, −60, −60, 0, 0] degrees.
Convert them to screw angles: theta_screw = hw2screw([30 -60 -60 -60 0 0]) returns [30, 30, −60, 30, 0, 0]. Confirm the result by hand with the formula above.
Run your FK on the screw angles: T = fk_space(S_list, M, theta_screw). S_list and M are in the workspace after Part 3.
The position is the last column of T: p_fk = T(1:3,4), in meters. For a UR3e the result must be (0.0984, −0.2008, 0.6328) m. If yours differs, find the mistake before starting Task 4.
Task 4: Compare with the Robot
⚠️ Tool offset: your FK model ends at the tool flange. The pendant reports the position of the active tool center point (TCP), which is the flange plus whatever tool offset is set on the robot. Find the TCP offset of your robot on the pendant (Installation, TCP) and enter it in MATLAB in meters as a column vector named tcp. For example, the Robotiq 2F-85 gripper set up in Lab 1 has a TCP offset of (0, 0, 174) mm, which gives tcp = [0; 0; 0.174]. A robot with no tool set has tcp = [0; 0; 0].
Jog the robot with the Teach Pendant (Move screen) to a pose of your choosing that is clear of the table, the walls and the camera. Move several joints, not just one.
Record the six joint angles shown on the pendant in Table 6 and convert them to screw angles: theta_screw = hw2screw([J1 J2 J3 J4 J5 J6]).
Compute the FK position of the TCP: T = fk_space(S_list, M, theta_screw), then p_fk = T(1:3,4) + T(1:3,1:3)*tcp. The second term carries the tool offset from the flange out to the TCP.
Read the TCP position from the pendant (Move screen, TCP position, with the feature set to Base) and convert millimeters to meters as a column vector: p_robot = [X; Y; Z] / 1000 (note the semicolons).
Compute the error in millimeters: err_mm = norm(p_fk - p_robot) * 1000. Record the angles, both positions and the error in Table 6.
Repeat for two more poses. Across the three poses every joint should have moved at least once.
How to read the result: an error under 3 mm is excellent and a few millimeters is a success, because the factory calibration of each robot differs slightly from the nominal link lengths. A large error that is about the same at every pose usually means the wrong tcp. An error near 1000 mm means one of the two vectors is a row instead of a column. The right size with the wrong sign in one coordinate means a sign error in one ω.
