[Heading1] Overview
In this lab you will derive the forward kinematics of a Universal Robots e-series 6-DOF manipulator (worked example: UR3e) using the Product of Exponentials (PoE) formulation from screw theory. Forward kinematics relates the values of the joint angles to the pose of the end effector. Your job is to derive the kinematic parameters (the six screw axes and the home pose matrix) from the robot's geometry, enter them into the MATLAB file, and verify the results against the physical robot.
[Heading1] Learning Objectives
[ListBullet] Identify the screw axis ωq for every joint of a 6-DOF manipulator from its physical geometry.
[ListBullet] Compute the 6x1 twist vector S=(ω,v) for each joint using v=−ω×q.
[ListBullet] Assemble the home pose matrix M from the robot's dimensions.
[ListBullet] Compute the final forward kinematic matrix T.
[ListBullet] Verify your FK model against the physical robot.
[Heading1] Background: The Product of Exponentials Formula
For a serial manipulator with n revolute joints, the space-frame PoE formula gives the end-effector pose as:
T(θ)=e[S1]θ1e[S2]θ2⋯e[S6]θ6M
Each factor e[Si]θi is a 4x4 transformation matrix that represents the effect of rotating joint i by angle θi. When θi is zero, that factor reduces to the 4x4 identity matrix. When all joint angles are zero, every exponential is the identity, so T=M.
The quantities you need to determine are:
[ListBullet] M: the 4x4 end-effector pose at the home configuration (all joint angles zero).
[ListBullet] </w:r><m:oMath><m:sSub><m:e><m:r><m:t>ωi: a 3x1 unit vector along the rotation axis of joint i.
[ListBullet] </w:r><m:oMath><m:sSub><m:e><m:r><m:t>qi: any point on the rotation axis of joint i.
[ListBullet] </w:r><m:oMath><m:sSub><m:e><m:r><m:t>vi=−ωi×qi: the linear velocity component, computed from ω and q via a cross product.
[ListBullet] </w:r><m:oMath><m:sSub><m:e><m:r><m:t>Si=[ωi;vi]: the 6x1 screw axis of joint i in the space frame at the home configuration.
The math that converts S and θ into a 4x4 matrix (the matrix exponential, using Rodrigues' formula) is provided for you in the MATLAB file. Your job is to figure out the correct Si and M from the robot's geometry.

[Heading2] Worked Example: The Screw Axis of Joint 2 (Shoulder)
Before filling in the table, let us derive one screw axis completely. We will use joint 2, the shoulder; every other joint follows the same three steps.
Step 1 - Find the rotation axis ω2.  In the home configuration (Figure 1), joint 2 rotates about a horizontal axis that points along the +y direction of the base frame. Since ω must be a unit vector: ω2=(0,1,0).
Step 2 - Pick a point q2 on the axis.  The shoulder axis crosses the base z-axis at height L1 above the origin, so a convenient choice is q2=(0,0,L1)=(0,0,0.15185) m for the UR3e. Any point on the axis will work; a convenient one keeps the arithmetic simple.
Step 3 - Compute v2=−ω2×q2.  Writing the cross product out component by component:
ω2×q2=(0,1,0)×(0,0,L1)=(1)(L1)−(0)(0),(0)(0)−(0)(L1),(0)(0)−(1)(0)=(L1,0,0)
so v2=−(L1,0,0)=(−L1,0,0)=(−0.15185,0,0).
</w:r><w:r><w:rPr><w:rFonts w:cs="Calibri"/></w:rPr><w:t xml:space="preserve">Stacking ω on top of v gives the 6x1 screw axis:  S2=(ω2,v2)=(0,1,0,−L1,0,0)=(0,1,0,−0.15185,0,0). The symbolic form is what you will enter in MATLAB, written with the code names from Table 1:  [0, 1, 0, -d1, 0, 0].
Physical check: v is the velocity of the point of the moving body that currently sits at the base origin. If the shoulder rotates at 1 rad/s about +y, a point at the origin (a distance L1 below the axis) sweeps in the -x direction at L1 m/s - exactly v2=(−L1,0,0).
Check your understanding: repeat Step 3 with a different point on the same axis, for example q=(0,0.3,L1). The extra +y component is parallel to ω2, so it drops out of the cross product and v2 is unchanged. If choosing a different point on the axis changes your v, something has gone wrong.
General cross product formula. For any two vectors a=(a₁,a₂,a₃) and b=(b₁,b₂,b₃): a×b=(a₂b₃−a₃b₂, a₃b₁−a₁b₃, a₁b₂−a₂b₁). The worked example above is this formula with a = ω and b = q. You will need it with ω along +y (joints 2, 3, 4, 6), along +z (joint 1) and along −z (joint 5).
Now derive the remaining five screw axes the same way and record them in Table 2. Joints 3, 4 and 6 are directly analogous to joint 2: ω=(0,1,0) and q on the base axis at the height of that joint. Read the heights from Figure 1: joint 3 is L1+L2 up, joint 4 is L1+L2+L3 up, and joint 6 is a further L5 above joint 4. Joint 1 is simpler still: its axis passes through the origin, so q = (0, 0, 0) and v = (0, 0, 0). Joint 5 is the one that catches people, so its first two steps are given here.
Joint 5 (wrist 2), Steps 1 and 2. Its axis is vertical and, in this lab, its positive direction is taken DOWNWARD: ω₅=(0,0,−1) (red arrow in Figure 1). The axis is offset from the base axis by L4 in the −y direction, so a convenient point on it is q₅=(0,−L₄,L₁+L₂+L₃); any height works because the axis is vertical. Step 3 is yours: apply the general formula to v₅=−ω₅×q₅. Physical check: with ω₅ pointing down, a point of the wrist at the base origin would move along +x, so v₅ should point along +x. Notice that flipping the sign of ω also flips v. If you pick ω₅ = (0, 0, +1) instead, the self-test for wrist 2 in Part 3 will show the x coordinate with the right size and the wrong sign.

[Heading2] How to Build the Rotation Matrix R
The rotation part of M answers one question: which way do the tool's axes point, written in base-frame coordinates? The columns of R are exactly the tool frame's x, y and z unit vectors expressed in the base frame: R = [x_tool, y_tool, z_tool].
Read the three directions from Figure 1 at the home pose. As a start: the tool's z-axis (pointing out of the flange) points along the base frame's −y direction, so the third column of R is (0, −1, 0). Determine x_tool and y_tool from the figure the same way. Take care with x_tool: Figure 1 marks it as pointing OUT of the page, while the base +x points INTO the page. Then check your result: a valid rotation matrix has columns of length 1, mutually perpendicular, with det(R) = +1.
Table 4 has one row per tool axis, but when you type M each of these vectors is a COLUMN: R = [x_tool, y_tool, z_tool], so R_x = x_tool goes down the first column of M, R_y = y_tool down the second and R_z = z_tool down the third, each written in base coordinates. (For this robot the correct R happens to be symmetric, so a transposed entry would still pass the self-tests. Get the convention right anyway; it matters for every other robot.)

[Heading2] Task 3: Determine the Angle Mapping
Turn on the robot arm and move it to the upright home position (Move screen, Smart Skills, Home). On the Move screen's joint panel, record the six joint angle readings. Expect roughly [0, −90, 0, −90, 0, 0] degrees; small deviations are normal, but a value that is off by 90 or 180 degrees means the arm is not at Home or you are reading a different panel.
theta_home = [___, ___, ___, ___, ___, ___]  (degrees)
Notice that the angles are not all zero. Our PoE formula assumes all joint angles are zero at the home configuration. There is a second difference: on joints 2 through 6 the pendant's positive rotation direction is opposite to the screw axes you derived. To see it, jog joint 2 a few degrees in the pendant's positive direction: the arm tips toward −x, whereas the right-hand rule about the red ω₂ = +y arrow of Figure 1 tips it toward +x. The conversion therefore needs a sign flip as well as an offset:
θhw(i)=s(i)θscrew(i)+θhome(i),s=[+1,−1,−1,−1,−1,−1]
